MEMORIES OF MY SCHOOL YEARS / 01

Before the Bell

Tiancheng Ma · Inner Mongolia, remembered

The exercise book lay open between Huang and me. He held down the left page with his elbow. I copied the last line, looked through the glass in the door, and moved my ruler beneath the next one.

“Not that answer,” he said. “The one below.”

A bucket scraped along the corridor. We put our own books over the open pages. The bucket passed. Huang lifted the corner of his book.

On the blackboard, someone had written the date: 2016. The sleeve of my school jacket covered the heel of my hand. I was fourteen.

On Fridays the boys from the First and Second Regiments brought their bags to the last lesson. The bags stood between their shoes. On Monday mornings there were washed socks hanging from the dormitory windows.

Feng was folding a worksheet into quarters.

“Are you going home?” I asked.

“My father’s in hospital.”

“What did they say?”

He pressed a thumbnail along the fold.

“They found it too late.”

Someone at the back called for a basketball. Feng folded the paper once more and put it in his pocket.

A few days later, the teacher paused at his name on the register. She drew a short line and read the next one. His desk was pushed against the wall. At the end of the week, another boy put a bag on it.

Wang stayed by the classroom window while the others carried their bags downstairs.

“You’re not going?” I said.

“There’s no hot water.”

He pulled at the collar of his shirt.

“You can’t even wash.”

Below us, a bus sounded its horn. Wang took one strap of his bag and put it over his shoulder.

Years later, his graduation photograph came through on my phone. Then a message saying he had started work. In the photograph, the edge of his white shirt showed above the gown.

On a day without school, Huang and I climbed the unfinished building. There were thirteen floors. We counted the landings. Dust lay on the steps, except where other shoes had crossed it.

At the top there was no glass in the windows. The wind moved through the empty rooms. Across the town, roofs flattened into strips of grey and red. A truck passed along the road below. Its engine reached us after it had gone behind a building.

Huang pointed out the school.

I put my hand against the concrete beside the window. There were small stones showing through it. When I took my hand away, my palm was white.

For the graduation photograph, they set benches in front of the school. Zhou sat at one end. A boy started towards the place beside her. Someone caught his sleeve. He stepped into the row behind.

The photographer raised his arm.

“Close up. There’s a gap.”

I sat beside Zhou. The bench shifted under us. She moved her hands from the wood to her knees.

“Look here.”

She turned towards the camera. I moved my foot out of the shadow of hers.

Ma wrote our surname in the corner of my notebook, beside mine.

“Same one,” he said.

“Our grandfathers are brothers.”

He added a second underline.

In high school his messages came after midnight. There would be a screenshot of a game, then another. I wrote back between two pages of homework. The next time I opened the conversation, my last sentence was still at the bottom.

The high-school mathematics teacher had our photographs clipped to a sheet. He ran a finger down it.

“Let’s have the most handsome one at the board. Tiancheng.”

Chairs scraped. Someone tapped my desk as I passed. My middle-school teacher had said it on the first day, too. This teacher handed me a piece of chalk.

The equation occupied two lines. I began a third.

Between lessons I read the winning essays from the New Concept Writing Competition. The paperback fitted inside my textbook. When the bell rang, I brought the textbook upright and kept one finger between the smaller pages.

The teacher turned towards the board. I lowered my eyes by a line.

After the placement examination, my name appeared on the advanced-class list. I carried my books down the corridor with my chin against the top one.

In the new room, I stopped beside a desk.

“Is anyone sitting here?”

A boy moved his coat.

For the first few lessons I wrote the teachers’ names at the tops of the pages. Later, there were only dates. Someone began borrowing my ruler. I left it between the desks.

For the television programme, a woman clipped a microphone to my collar. She tucked the cable inside my jacket and stepped back. Beyond her, a man held up three fingers, then two.

The ball struck the rim. I came down with the others. One shoe turned against the court. The ball bounced twice before someone caught it.

For months I went up the school stairs one step at a time, bringing both feet onto each tread. At the bell, shoes passed on either side. I kept a hand on the rail.

At my desk I pulled the chair back farther than before. My leg lay under the next desk. When someone needed to pass, I lifted it with both hands.

The paperback stayed in my bag. Its cover had curled around the exercise books. Returned test papers lay on top. I folded each one with the mark inside.

By the entrance examination, I was still taking the stairs with a hand on the rail.

In the examination room, I put my identity card on the upper corner of the desk. The invigilator checked it against my face. Outside the windows, someone was dragging a chair across a floor.

I wrote my name in the boxes. The last letter touched the printed edge. I moved the card a little to the left.
