MEMORIES OF MY SCHOOL YEARS / 01

The Thirteenth Floor

By Tiancheng Ma · A fictionalised memoir · 7,878 words · About 40 minutes

An account of classrooms, borrowed answers, unfinished buildings, and the people a timetable could not keep together.

Based on my memories. Dialogue, physical details, and some scenes are imaginatively reconstructed. Names are abbreviated. Unknown later events remain unknown; imagined commentators are satirical inventions.

01 / An Inventory of the Entrance

Before I put anyone back into that classroom, I should put back the door.

In the version I can still reach, its lower half is painted a green that has stopped believing in green. Near the handle, the paint has worn through to a darker surface. A little farther down there is a pale diagonal scratch. It might have been made by a desk being carried out. It might have been made by a boy with nothing to do. I cannot identify the boy. There were many qualified candidates.

The door closes badly. You have to lift it a little, then pull. The sound is too large for the action. A small private departure becomes a public announcement.

Inside, the desks face the blackboard in rows. This arrangement is presented as natural, like the direction in which a river flows. A river, however, occasionally has the courage to turn. Our desks have grooves along their upper edges for pens. The grooves also hold grey dust, pencil shavings, and those tiny balls of rubber left by erasers. Years of correction have accumulated there. Nobody has yet found a use for them.

Somebody has scratched a name into the wood. Another person has scratched through it. The second operation has made the name harder to read and much harder to forget. This is a useful introduction to censorship, although it is not on the timetable.

At the front stands the teacher's desk. Its position gives it the air of a small customs office. On one side are questions. On the other side are answers that have already been approved. A stack of exercise books waits at the edge, held flat by its own weight. The top one has a curled corner. There is a pen beside it, uncapped. I remember that sort of pen more clearly than I remember entire weeks.

The windows look onto a yard. Their metal frames collect dirt in the corners. The glass has been wiped with something that leaves wide, faint strokes. In strong light, the strokes become visible and the world outside looks as though someone has attempted to erase it without finishing. A strip of sunlight reaches the second row before it reaches the third. Dust drifts through it with the freedom of something that cannot be given homework.

The radiator is under the window. Whether it was warm on a particular morning is beyond me. Memory has preserved its outline and neglected its performance. This is how most institutions prefer to be remembered.

Outside the gate, the street widens in my mind around small shops. There are wrappers near a wall, a bicycle leaning at an angle its owner will call perfectly safe, and a patch of pavement that darkens when wet. A drain has a broken edge. Shoes learn to avoid it before their owners learn the dates of historical treaties. The practical curriculum is always running beside the official one. It does not issue certificates.

A shop window holds exercise books, pens, cheap snacks. The packets are brighter than anything in the classroom. They promise flavour without requiring a statement of personal ambition. A faded advertisement hangs crookedly. Above it, newer signs make newer promises. The town keeps acquiring the future in rectangular pieces. The screws are sometimes reused.

On a school morning, you can tell the time by the density of shoulders near the gate. Early, there is room to walk around a puddle. Later, you step into it. Adults will explain that time is an abstract resource. At fourteen, I know it as the difference between dry socks and wet ones.

I could go on describing the place until nobody is left to enter it. There is a temptation in this. Buildings are easier company than former classmates. They do not get married without telling you. They do not leave school in the middle of a week. They stand where you put them, provided you are willing to overlook demolition.

Still, someone has to come through the door.

I think the year was 2016. I was fourteen. The town was in Inner Mongolia, in the region of Bayannur. The rest of the map can remain a little blurred. I have no wish to improve an uncertain memory by giving it a postcode.

Huang arrived early. So did I.

From the street, we must have looked like two boys taking responsibility for their future. Our schoolbags were on our backs. Our faces were serious. The morning had not yet been wasted.

We had homework to copy.

02 / Original Work

Huang put the borrowed exercise book between us. It lay open with an obedient flatness that suggested its owner had completed it at a reasonable hour.

I copied the first line. Then I looked at the door.

“You watch,” I said.

“I'm writing.”

“Then write faster.”

He looked up. I looked down. For a few seconds we achieved a division of labour that would have pleased a management consultant. Then a sound came from the corridor and both of us looked up. Production stopped. The consultant would have recommended a meeting.

I was a good student. I understood much of what we were being taught. I could answer questions and do well in examinations. I also copied homework. These facts lived together without much difficulty. It was only the adults' descriptions of children that required separate apartments.

A good student was supposed to be a person who did good-student things all day. A bad student was someone whose actions arrived already labelled. If I was tired, I had worked hard. If another boy was tired, he lacked discipline. The same closed eyelids could lead to quite different diagnoses. No specialist equipment was necessary. The last examination result would do.

We were given homework every day. Some of it taught us something. Some of it confirmed that our hands remained attached to our arms. By morning, the useful and the useless parts had acquired equal authority because both were due.

“Don't copy that mistake,” Huang whispered.

“Which one?”

“I don't know. There must be one.”

We did not want identical work. Identical knowledge was the educational ideal, but identical handwriting aroused suspicion. I shortened a sentence. Huang changed a word. Here, at last, was an incentive to develop an individual voice.

There was a bottle of water on the desk. It had cooled overnight. I drank without thinking and felt the cold descend through my chest. Outside the window, the light was coming up. Somebody in the corridor was dragging a foot. We listened until the sound passed.

Years later, I could sell this scene differently. I could say that, at fourteen, I had already disrupted the traditional knowledge pipeline. I had built a collaborative platform. I had optimised delivery under severe time constraints. The future had arrived, wearing a school uniform and borrowing another boy's solutions.

Invest in me. I understand scale. One completed workbook can serve an entire ecosystem.

This is why I distrust a heroic account of my own beginnings. Change the nouns and misconduct becomes a keynote speech. Leave the nouns alone and there are two children trying not to get caught.

I prefer the children. They are not especially admirable, but they are available for questioning.

When the room filled, we returned the exercise book. Chairs scraped. Someone asked to borrow an eraser. Huang moved back to his seat. I arranged my books so that the completed work was visible.

The teacher came in. The day began with the ordinary miracle of everybody appearing to have been ready all along.

03 / Places with Numbers

Some boys in our class came from settlements outside the county town. I heard names like First Regiment and Second Regiment. I did not understand why a place where people lived should sound like an instruction to march. I still cannot provide the history of those names from what I knew at fourteen.

To me, the names meant distance.

They meant that a boy could not simply walk home after the last lesson. He was a boarder. He slept at school and waited for the weekend. On Friday, a bag that had spent the week under a bed was brought out again. On Sunday, it came back with its shape slightly altered. Laundry had been exchanged. Food might have been added. A whole home could announce its existence through the corner of a plastic bag.

Not every boy from those places had the same life. At fourteen, I was careless about this. I grouped people by the things I could see: shoes, marks, the frequency with which a teacher said their names angrily. Poverty makes an efficient label because people with enough money can apply it from a distance.

Some had little money. Some had difficulties at home. Some seemed to have decided that school was a game they had already lost. They stopped handing in work. They stopped listening. They laughed at the wrong time, which was sometimes the only time available to them.

We called this giving up.

It is an economical phrase. It saves the speaker from finding out what has been carrying the weight.

I do not want to improve those boys into saints. They could be irritating. They could be cruel. So could I, with better marks and a more acceptable explanation. A difficult life does not make every action right. It does make the action less useful as a complete biography.

There is a kind of educational expert who can describe a child for twenty minutes without mentioning a bed, a meal, a parent, or the journey home. Such a person has achieved an impressive level of abstraction. If asked why a plant is dying, he will discuss its insufficient commitment to photosynthesis.

I am constructing this expert now. He is a composite, a man assembled from tones of voice. He need not have stood in our classroom. We could do most of his work ourselves.

“He just doesn't care.”

I probably said that about someone.

It sounds less cruel when said by a boy. It is still a way of closing a door.

On Friday, the boarders prepared to leave. Those of us who lived closer treated departure as an ordinary extension of the day. For them it required a bag and a plan. I could not have drawn all their routes on a map. I knew which answers they had left blank in a test. This was the kind of information our lives together made easy to obtain.

The school knew how to rank us. It did not always know how far we had travelled to be ranked.

04 / Hot Water

Wang complained about his family. He did not do it in the polished language in which adults discuss an unfortunate background. He said he had been born badly. He said his parents did not look after him. The words came out with the irritation of someone reporting a defect that everyone expected him to repair himself.

“I don't even want to go home,” he said.

“Why?”

“There's no hot water. I can't have a proper wash.”

The exact sentences have gone. That is how I put them together now. What remains without assistance is the hot water.

I remember it because it was small enough to understand. Family unhappiness was a room with no light switch. Hot water was a tap. A person could point to its absence. Here was something that ought to come out and did not.

At school, we were told to have ambitions. These were generally pictured at a useful distance: university, a profession, a transformed life. Wang wanted to wash. It was not a competitive answer. Nobody was going to put it on a banner.

Yet there was more exact knowledge in that complaint than in many speeches about aspiration. A life can be made miserable by things too ordinary to receive a title. A basin. A cold room. The prospect of taking off your clothes when you already cannot get warm. A journey home that offers no relief from being tired.

I cannot remember my reply. I may have said something sensible. I may have said something useless in a sensible voice. I am reluctant to award myself the better option simply because I am the one writing.

This is a difficulty with first-person stories. The narrator controls the lighting. He can leave his own mean little gestures in the dark and illuminate the one occasion on which he offered somebody a chair. By the end, he looks like a decent person who has survived a badly managed world. Sometimes he has merely survived his editor.

Wang later finished an undergraduate degree. He works now. Those are the facts that reached me.

I am pleased about them. I do not know enough about his present life to make them carry more than they can. Employment is not a certificate that childhood has been repaired. A degree does not travel backwards and heat the water.

The motivational version would be easy. Boy from hardship defeats fate. Against all odds, he reconstructs his future. Put his younger face on the left and his graduation photograph on the right. Between them, sell a method.

But Wang did the living. The rest of us should be careful about charging admission.

Sometimes I think of asking him about those days. Then I imagine the message arriving while he is working. A man has an ordinary afternoon to get through. An old classmate appears, carrying a basin from 2016.

So I leave the question unsent.

I hope he has hot water. That is all. It is not a conclusion about the human spirit. A functioning water heater would be enough.

05 / A Few Days

Feng told me about his father at school.

In my reconstruction, we are near a desk. There is a book between us, though neither of us is reading it. Feng touches the corner of a page and lets it go. I give him this action because I remember the helplessness of the conversation more clearly than the placement of his hands. People need somewhere to put their hands when words are no use.

“My father's ill,” he says.

“How ill?”

“They found it late.”

Then, after a moment, something about there being very little they could do.

I had heard of serious illness. Adults spoke of it with lowered voices, as if volume were one of the available treatments. I knew the outline of what Feng was saying. His father had been diagnosed with an illness that was already at a terminal stage. This was not an excuse for unfinished homework. It was a fact from a larger world that had entered our room without asking the teacher.

“Will you go home?” I ask in the conversation I can now make out.

“I don't know.”

He may have known. He may have told me. I have kept the uncertainty because certainty would belong to him, and I no longer have a way to ask for it.

The lesson continued, or another lesson began. Somebody wanted a ruler. A chair leg caught on the floor. The world can receive terrible news and still require a straight line to be drawn in an exercise book.

A few days later, Feng left school.

This is where a more competent novelist would follow him. There would be a bus journey, a doorway, a father on a bed. A scene would arrive with enough detail to make a reader cry. I could invent all of it. I could make the dust on his shoes correspond to the dust on his father's window. I could arrange the light. I could be very moving at somebody else's expense.

I do not know what happened in those rooms.

I know he left. I know I never saw him again.

For a while, absence is still attached to a person. You expect him to come through the door. Then the expectation loosens. Somebody moves a book into the available space. The class gets used to its new size without holding a meeting about it.

Schools are built to notice departures of a certain kind. A student entering a good university can become a photograph on a wall. A boy leaving because his father is desperately ill is harder to display. The event does not support a slogan. It offers no convenient proof that the system is working.

I cannot tell you what help Feng received, if any. I cannot fill that gap with either a defence or an accusation. What I can describe is the smallness of my own knowledge. I sat in the same room as him and knew almost nothing about the life that could take him out of it.

Huang and I continued to arrive early. The teachers continued to set work. I continued to care about my marks. This seems heartless when written in a row. At the time, it was simply the next morning.

The timetable survived him without a scratch.

I am not sure there is a kind way to write that sentence. There was no dramatic way to live it either. The bell rang. We opened our books.

06 / The Thirteenth Floor

On days off, Huang and I sometimes climbed an abandoned building.

It had thirteen floors. That number, unlike many more useful facts, has remained intact. There were no lessons at the top. Nobody was taking attendance. We went because we were bored and the view was good. We also had the adolescent belief that an unlocked entrance was a form of permission. It was not.

The staircase was made of bare concrete. Dust gathered where each tread met the wall. In places, the surface had a roughness that caught the light differently from the surrounding grey. There were openings intended for windows, and openings that seemed to have no settled purpose. The building had reached the stage at which you could recognise its ambition without being able to live in it.

On a lower floor, a length of wire emerged from the wall and bent down. It resembled a question that had been asked too often. A patch of mortar had dried in a shape that nobody would bother naming. From outside, the structure was one building. Inside, it was a collection of unfinished decisions.

We did not know why it had been abandoned. We supplied explanations with the confidence of boys who had never paid for cement.

“They ran out of money,” one of us said.

“Then why did they build thirteen floors?”

“They had money for twelve and a bit.”

This was our first theory of property development. Later, more expensive theories would use words like liquidity.

The higher we went, the less the town sounded like itself. Individual voices disappeared first. Traffic became a continuous murmur. A dog might still be heard, but without a visible dog the bark seemed to come from the town as a whole. We paused on a landing. Huang went on. His steps sounded above me before I could see him again.

At the top, the air moved through the empty space with no respect for the proposed floor plan.

There were roofs below us. Roads. Walls dividing one modest enclosure from another. Beyond them, the land widened. The school became a shape among shapes. From there, its importance was difficult to prove.

“Which way is your house?” Huang asked.

I pointed.

“That one?”

“Somewhere there.”

This was the accuracy our future plans also possessed.

I could not have pointed to Feng's home. I could not have pointed to Wang's. Their places were present in the landscape and absent from my knowledge. Height had improved the view. It had not made me wise.

There was a brief satisfaction in being above the streets that normally contained us. It was not freedom in any substantial sense. We still had to go down. We still had school. If we became hungry, a philosophical attitude would not buy noodles.

But nothing at the top was asking us to become someone. For a while, that was sufficient.

If I were writing the story of a young founder, I would call this my first encounter with the horizon. I would say I saw an industry waiting to be disrupted. I would stand in front of a screen and explain that vision means going where others fear to climb.

In fact, I remember the wind pulling at a loose part of my shirt. I remember Huang being there. I remember that the descent took time because the building possessed exactly as many stairs on the way down.

Back then, my legs managed them without comment.

07 / The Empty Place Beside Zhou

For our middle-school graduation photograph, the class had to become orderly enough to fit into a rectangle.

This took some time. Taller students were moved. Shorter students were called forward. Somebody tried to stand beside a friend and was sent elsewhere. There is a practical democracy to a camera: it wants your face visible, regardless of your preferred alliances.

Zhou had difficulty with things the rest of us were expected to find easy. I cannot supply a diagnosis. We were children. Our vocabulary for difference was both limited and cruel. Some of the other students mocked her. Avoiding her had become a habit, and habits are particularly hard to question when a group shares them.

There was a place beside her. Nobody seemed eager to take it.

This was not announced. Nobody stood up and proposed a policy. That is how much of ordinary exclusion works. A small movement away. A face turned towards somebody else. A vacant place that everyone can see and everyone treats as an accident.

I sat beside her.

I would like to stop the account there, before I can make a statue of myself out of a chair.

On a personal website, this is dangerous material. It fits neatly into the section where a man demonstrates that he was decent before he became interesting. Add a swelling soundtrack. Call the incident a stand against the system. Describe the courage it took to occupy a seat that was already available.

It took very little.

Zhou was the one who had been living with the treatment. She had not been waiting for a scene in my development. Whatever the gesture meant to her belongs to her. I cannot write her gratitude into the space where I have failed to remember her words.

I remember sitting down. I remember that we all had to face the same direction. I imagine the tiny adjustments before the picture: a shoulder lifted, a sleeve pulled straight, somebody being told to stop moving. The photographer needed a still moment. We were able to provide one.

A class photograph is a flattering document. It makes people look as though they have agreed to be together. All the small refusals happen just outside the exposure. In later years, we can point to the picture and say, “Our class,” with the satisfaction of having evidence.

The evidence proves that we were in one place at one time. It cannot say how we treated one another before the shutter opened.

I wonder where Zhou is now. I have no answer. I do not want to give her an imaginary future merely to settle an old discomfort of mine. A successful career would not justify what happened. A difficult life would not make the cruelty more wrong than it already was.

The photograph was taken. We got up.

After all the trouble of arranging us, it took almost no time for us to separate.

08 / A Family Resemblance

There was another Ma in my life at school. Our grandfathers were brothers. We shared a surname and a small piece of family history, which at that age seemed like an unusually good reason to be friends.

We did not have to establish a connection. Adults had built it for us years before we arrived. We could simply use it. There was comfort in that. In a classroom where so much depended on how well you performed, here was a relationship you could enter by answering to your own name.

“Another Ma,” somebody would say.

“We have more,” I could have replied. “The family is expanding.”

It was an enterprise with no business plan and a fairly durable brand.

We were close in middle school. Later, in high school, he spent more and more time playing games. That is part of what I remember. It is not enough to explain his life, though it would be very convenient if it were.

Adults like a single cause. Games. Bad company. A weak will. One word can be carried around more easily than a person. Once it has been attached, you can stop asking questions and still feel well informed.

I know that games became important to him. I do not know everything they gave him. Perhaps there was pleasure in them. Perhaps there was company. Perhaps a world with clear rules was preferable, for a few hours, to one in which adults changed the rules and called it character building. These are possibilities, not information I received from him.

We lost touch.

There was no argument large enough to preserve itself. No accusation. No final sentence with the useful shape of an ending. One conversation was followed by a longer interval. Then another. Eventually the interval became what the relationship consisted of.

Our grandfathers have been dead for years now. The fact that they were brothers remains true. Family trees are excellent at preserving connections that daily life no longer services. On paper, we can still stand beside each other indefinitely. Paper has no shift to finish and no messages it means to answer later.

I cannot remember the last thing I said to Ma. It was probably not worth remembering at the time. That is one of the unpleasant efficiencies of an ordinary day. It can contain an ending without allocating any extra time for it.

Sometimes I think I should contact him. Then I consider what the first message would have to carry. Our surname. Our grandfathers. All the years we have not discussed. A simple “How are you?” becomes a small truck with too much furniture on it.

Still, the distance is not an order. It is something we have allowed to happen. Calling it a law of life makes it sound less like a message I have not sent.

I have not sent it.

09 / The Best-Looking Boy

At the start of high school, I was happy.

It is worth saying this plainly. An unhappy ending has a way of reaching backwards and confiscating the earlier good days. I do not want to help it. The first semester was good. I made friends. I got along with my classmates. I did well in my work. There were mornings when I went to school without needing to negotiate with myself first.

Our mathematics teacher had a sheet containing our information and photographs. I remember the photographs because they gave him an alternative method of selection.

One day, he looked down at the sheet.

“Let's have the best-looking one come up and do this problem,” he said.

Then: “Tiancheng. Come on.”

For a moment I had achieved distinction in a subject I had not prepared. I stood up with what I hope was a tolerable expression. There is no elegant way to accept a public compliment at that age. Deny it too vigorously and you appear to be fishing. Accept it too comfortably and your classmates will make sure you never hear the end of it.

The blackboard solved this problem by presenting me with another one.

I had to do the mathematics.

I no longer remember the question. My vanity has carefully retained the selection process and discarded the academic outcome. It is a specialised archive.

During middle school and high school, I was not short of admirers. I would be dishonest if I pretended this was a burden I carried with great maturity. It pleased me. I was a good student, and apparently I was also pleasant to look at. Life seemed to have made a favourable initial offer. I had not yet read the remaining terms.

There is tenderness in remembering a younger self who has not learned to distrust a good day. There is also the urge to tell him to stop looking quite so satisfied. I experience both. The second feeling is probably envy wearing a sensible coat.

During breaks, I read winning pieces from the New Concept Writing Competition. The pages offered a different kind of room from the one around me. In them, young people were permitted to notice things that would not improve a test score. A street could matter because someone had once walked down it. A rainy afternoon could occupy several paragraphs without being required to irrigate anything.

Sometimes the bell rang while I was still inside a sentence.

I placed a textbook over the collection and kept reading where I could. The teacher spoke. I lowered my eyes with an appearance of attention that was not entirely false. I was attending closely to something. We disagreed only about the object.

My thumb held a page in place. A little triangle of it protruded beneath the textbook. I waited for a suitable moment to turn it. This was an education in timing, if not the one being offered.

“What are you reading?” someone asked during a break.

“Something good.”

“Will it be on the test?”

“I hope not.”

I enjoyed the pressure of the classroom less than I enjoyed having things to do well. These were not always the same thing, but at first I could manage both. There was schoolwork. There were people who liked me. There were books. When a lesson ended, the next few minutes still felt like property I owned.

I did not call that happiness at the time. I was occupied with it.

10 / The Better Room

In the second semester, an examination divided us again. There were advanced classes and ordinary classes. I was placed in an advanced one.

The vocabulary did much of the work. “Ordinary” is a perfectly respectable word until someone uses it as the alternative to where you are supposed to want to be. Then it acquires a slight smell of disappointment. A child can discover that his classroom has become an adjective.

The decision had been made through marks. Marks possess the great advantage of looking innocent on paper. They do not raise their voices. They simply stand there in a column while people rearrange their opinion of you.

I carried my books to the new room.

This should have felt like a promotion. For a while, it felt like being sent to somebody else's table halfway through dinner. There were unfamiliar faces, unfamiliar teachers, and small established ways of doing things that I did not yet know. During breaks, I had nowhere obvious to direct a remark. I stayed in my seat more than I had before.

In the old class, I could turn around and expect someone to understand why I was smiling. In the new one, even a joke required background information. Friendship is partly the luxury of not having to introduce your own sentences.

“You'll settle in,” people say.

They are often right. It is still an unhelpful description of the hours before it happens.

At fourteen or fifteen, a classroom is not merely a location. It is the place where your face has an agreed meaning. You are the funny one, the quiet one, the one who lends pens, the one whom the mathematics teacher publicly considers handsome. Move the desks and the face has to apply for recognition again.

I gradually learned the new names. I learned whose silence was friendly and whose silence was just silence. There were small exchanges. A question about work. A borrowed object returned. A remark that received a laugh without needing an explanation. The room became less like a room I had entered by mistake.

My work improved. I found my footing.

This is where I could introduce the language of conquest. I rebuilt my position. I disrupted the established hierarchy. I captured attention in a competitive market. Watch the young Tiancheng rise! The old order had failed to account for my potential!

In practice, I had started knowing who to ask for an eraser.

The grand language is tempting because it makes adaptation sound voluntary. It gives the boy a sword and removes the possibility that he was lonely. But the lonely boy did the actual work. He came in again the next morning. He listened for a place to join the conversation. He made a few friends.

I do not want to deprive him of that by declaring him a visionary.

We were told we had earned our place. In one sense, we had: we had taken the examination and received the necessary marks. The statement became less reliable when extended into a complete theory of human value. Feng had also once occupied a desk. His departure had not been decided by a fair comparison of our potential.

I liked doing well. I still do. What troubles me is how easily liking a good result can become believing that every result is deserved.

11 / On Behalf of the School

A teacher chose me to represent the school on a television programme.

I was pleased. There is no need to excavate a more complex motive. I had been selected, and selection felt good. The same machinery I am capable of criticising from a comfortable distance had turned in my direction and produced an invitation. I accepted.

In the scene I reconstruct, somebody fixes a microphone to my clothes. The clip pulls slightly at the fabric. I am told not to touch it. This immediately makes touching it seem like the only natural thing my hand could do.

The programme's precise staging has not survived as clearly as the feeling of being there. I will not manufacture a title, an award, or a national audience. A school had sent me to appear on its behalf. At that age, the distinction was large enough without additional lighting.

“Stand here.”

“Here?”

“A little to the side.”

You can make a philosophical discovery while moving six inches: visibility is often somebody else's arrangement.

I tried to look capable. I hoped to say the right thing. I was proud that a teacher trusted me with the task. That trust was real, even if institutions also enjoy collecting presentable children as evidence of their own effectiveness.

A school is not one person. It contains the teacher who encourages you, the rule that exhausts you, the adult who makes time, and the procedure that has none. To describe all of it as one villain would be as lazy as printing “excellent” on the entire building. The teachers who treated me kindly do not disappear because I have learned to make jokes about the system around them.

Still, a camera changes the balance of things. It can make a clean corner stand for a whole room. It can take a boy who happens to be doing well and present him as the natural product of a reliable process. Somewhere beyond the frame are children whose lives would make the explanation less tidy.

I was happy to be in the frame.

Later, an adult imagination suggests a campaign slogan: THE FUTURE HAS ARRIVED. There I am, representing a new generation, ready to reconstruct everything. First the school, then the market, then perhaps civilisation, subject to available funding. Behind every stirring sentence there ought to be a subscription button.

Youth makes excellent promotional material. It has not yet accumulated enough details to contradict the pitch.

But that afternoon was also just an afternoon. The microphone had to be removed. Clothes settled back into place. I went on with my school life. A public appearance did not cancel the next assignment.

For the first part of high school, I was managing. The workload was heavy. The expectations were high. I found ways through. There were good days that deserve to remain good days even now.

I played basketball.

For a while, that sentence requires nothing after it.

12 / The Distance to the Door

Then I got hurt playing.

I am tempted to prepare the moment with weather. A hard blue sky. A ball hanging in the air. The squeak of a shoe. It would make the event feel inevitable, as if the whole afternoon had been moving towards my injury with the care of a film crew.

It did not feel inevitable from inside it.

There was basketball, and then there was an injury. For several months, I could not walk normally. I did not receive surgery at that time. Before I finished high school, I had never fully recovered. I cannot improve this account by naming a medical diagnosis I have not supplied or assigning motives to decisions whose full circumstances are absent from these pages.

What I can describe is the change in scale.

The school became larger.

The distance from a desk to a door stopped being empty space. It contained effort, time, and the calculation of whether I really needed to get up. Stairs acquired individual personalities. A corridor that used to be a route became something to be completed.

On the thirteenth floor with Huang, I had treated height as a free gift. Now a few steps could make demands. The body keeps no archive of promises. It is not impressed by what you managed last term.

At the bell, chairs moved back. People stood. I waited for some of them to pass. Their speed was ordinary. That was the difficulty. Nobody had to run for me to fall behind.

Someone might say, “Coming?”

“In a minute.”

The phrase did a great deal of work. It reassured the other person. It protected me from explaining. It suggested that the delay was small and would soon be over. Sometimes a minute was enough. Sometimes it was the name I gave to something much longer.

My life and my studies began to go downhill. I do not remember a single examination that formally announced the change. Work accumulated. My marks fell. Things that had once been manageable began to cost more energy than I had.

An unfinished exercise lay on the desk. By the next day there was another. I had once dealt with unfinished homework by arriving early with Huang. That method belonged to a simpler shortage. Now the missing thing was not merely an answer.

I missed the ease with which I used to inhabit a day.

That is an awkward loss to explain. You can point to an injury. It is harder to point to the old, unthinking assumption that you will stand up when you want to stand up, arrive when the others arrive, and still have something left for the lesson.

Advice becomes plentiful when useful help is difficult. Some advice is kindly meant. Some of it allows the speaker to leave feeling that a contribution has been made. The cheapest form is an instruction to be positive. It requires no adjustment to the stairs.

Imagine a committee examining a broken chair. One member recommends greater resilience on the part of the sitter. Another suggests a campaign celebrating the opportunities created by falling. A third proposes measuring attitude before and after contact with the floor. The report is excellent. The chair remains broken.

This committee did not convene at my school. I convene it here because I recognise its reasoning in the stories we tell about endurance, including the ones I am tempted to tell about myself.

I could call those years my crucible. I could say pain forged an unbreakable will. I could place every difficulty on a rising graph and label it an investment in future strength. Then the suffering would have a return. The audience could relax. Nothing had been wasted.

But I would have preferred to walk normally.

I would have preferred to keep up without having to become an example of anything.

Pain did not make me a better person on schedule.

There were days when it made me tired. Days when I was less patient. Days when the future shrank to the next manageable task. I do not think this is a failure of interpretation. A difficult period is permitted to be difficult without eventually submitting an educational outcome.

Still, I continued.

That sentence is smaller than the heroic version, but it is not nothing. I went through the remaining days of high school. I worked as well as I could, including on days when that was not very well. I held on to the prospect of the university entrance examination because it was there, because everyone was moving towards it, because I did not know another route I could take at the time.

The gaokao approached with the peculiar authority of a date that will not change to accommodate you. A calendar is a simple machine. It cannot be accused of taking pleasure in your circumstances. It moves forward regardless, which in practice can feel similar.

I sat the examination.

There was paper. There were questions. There was the familiar business of getting something from my head onto a page before time ran out. After all the large things attached to it, the act itself still depended on a pen.

I got into university.

Here is the place for applause. The young man has defied fate. He has crossed the threshold. The system has been conquered, or vindicated, depending on who commissioned the speech. Music rises. The camera finds his face.

I was relieved. I was also tired.

The admission did not reach back into the previous months and make them easier. It gave me somewhere to go next. That was a great deal. It was not everything.

I wish we were better at allowing a good outcome to be good without requiring it to excuse the entire journey.

13 / The People Outside the Frame

I am in Germany now.

There is a room here in which I can sit and think about those rooms. The distance is large enough to invite symbolism. Inner Mongolia. Germany. A boy crossing continents, rebuilding himself, moving towards the future. I could put these places on a line and pretend the line explains me.

Mostly, it explains why the time on my phone is different from the time in old classmates' lives.

I have lost touch with almost everyone from middle school and high school. I sometimes say ninety-nine per cent. I have never counted. It is a number chosen for the feeling, like a price ending in nine. It gives the impression of calculation while making an emotional offer.

People are busy. They work. They have obligations I know nothing about. Their ordinary mornings would need an introduction if they described them to me. Mine would need one too. We once shared a timetable. Now arranging a conversation can feel like negotiating between small, exhausted countries.

“We should catch up.”

“Yes. When things are less busy.”

The second sentence is usually sincere. That is why it is so effective.

At school, friendship often happened without an appointment. Someone was there when you turned around. A remark could be made because the other person already knew the preceding ten minutes. As our lives separate, the ten minutes become ten years. We do not necessarily love each other less. We lose the machinery that made affection easy to practise.

This sounds like a law. Perhaps it is only a description of what I have allowed to become normal. There is comfort in calling a neglected relationship inevitable. It releases both parties from the small embarrassment of beginning again.

I do not have a solution. I have a phone.

Wang has graduated and is working. Feng remains on the far side of a departure I cannot fill in. Ma still shares my surname. Our grandfathers are still brothers, although both are long dead. Zhou has a life that does not depend on whether I remember to mention her. Huang is still, in one part of my mind, a few steps above me on a concrete staircase.

They return in different roles when I tell the story. The lookout over an exercise book becomes the boy at the top of a building. A classmate complaining about a bath becomes a man with a job. A girl beside an empty seat becomes a question about what a photograph leaves out. No one remains neatly in the chapter where I first put them.

Neither do I.

I am the boy who copied. The boy who did well. The boy who liked being called handsome. The boy who sat beside Zhou. The boy who did not know what to say to Feng. The boy who found a new place in a new class. The boy who could not walk properly and kept going anyway. Putting these boys together does not produce a hero. It produces the person responsible for this page.

I have even put a “Vote for me” button on my website. First I intend to become fluent in German. Then, perhaps, a mayor. It is good to have a public ambition large enough to distract from an unfinished grammar exercise.

There is a familiar promotional voice available for this, too. From a county classroom to the global stage! From adversity to leadership! The future is already here, and it has a personal brand!

I hear the voice. Sometimes I borrow it for fun. I do not want it to have the last word about Feng, or Wang, or the stairs.

A story like this can also be sold in smaller pieces. A bold sentence about education. A clip about failure. A picture of an empty desk. The internet can turn almost any sadness into a clean rectangle with room beneath it for reactions. I know the temptation. I have used short paragraphs. I have put certain sentences in bold. I would like you to keep reading.

But attention is not the same as remembering someone. It can leave as quickly as it arrived. If you remember only one thing about Wang, I would rather it were the hot water than a slogan about overcoming poverty. If you remember Feng, leave a little room for the life I do not know.

Sometimes, late in the day, the light in my room reaches the edge of a cup. The drink has gone cold. For a moment it resembles another cold drink, on another desk, beside an exercise book I was not supposed to be using.

Nothing extraordinary happens. No message arrives from the past. The dead do not return. An old friend does not suddenly call because a paragraph has become sufficiently sincere.

I lift the cup. I consider reheating it. Then I put it down and read the sentence again.

In the building with thirteen floors, Huang has already reached the next landing. There is dust where the stairs meet the wall. The opening above him is pale with daylight. We have no useful reason to be there, which is part of why I remember it so well.

He looks back.

“Coming?”

I am coming. In that small, unfinished part of my life, I still can.

We get to the top. The town is spread below us. For a while neither of us says anything.

Then one of us gets hungry.
